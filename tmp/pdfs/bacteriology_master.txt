PART I - ESSAY / ORAL DISCUSSION QUESTIONS
1. Discuss the contributions of important scientists to microbiology and bacteriology.
Answer: Lucretius and Girolamo Fracastoro proposed that disease could be caused by invisible living creatures.
Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was the first true microbiologist and Father of Bacteriology and Protozoology. Louis
Pasteur proposed heat for killing bacteria and used attenuated cultures as vaccines. Robert Koch advanced germ
theory and discovered Bacillus anthracis and Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Ignaz Semmelweis is associated with
handwashing and hospital hygiene; Joseph Lister used phenol as an antimicrobial; Carl von Linne introduced
binomial nomenclature; Hans Christian Gram developed the Gram stain; Julius Richard Petri developed the Petri
plate; Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin; Paul Ehrlich discovered salvarsan for syphilis.
2. Explain bacterial taxonomy, nomenclature, and identification.
Answer: Taxonomy is classification, identification, and naming. Hierarchy: Domain -> Kingdom -> Division/Phylum
-> Class -> Order -> Family -> Genus -> Species. Mnemonic: Dear King David Came Over For Good Soup.
Subspecies may be differentiated by strain (genetic), serotype/serovar (antigenic), biotype/biovar (biochemical),
and morphovar (morphological). Binomial nomenclature uses Genus + specific epithet, e.g., Escherichia coli.
Identification may be genotypic (DNA sequencing, PCR, whole-genome sequencing) or phenotypic (microscopy,
staining, culture, biochemical tests, serology).
3. Compare Gram-positive, Gram-negative, acid-fast, and cell-wall-deficient bacteria.
Answer: Gram-positive bacteria have thick peptidoglycan and teichoic acids and retain crystal violet (purple).
Gram-negative bacteria have thin peptidoglycan plus an outer membrane containing LPS and appear pink/red after
counterstaining. Acid-fast bacteria have lipid-rich, mycolic-acid-containing walls and resist acid-alcohol
decolorization; examples include M. tuberculosis and M. leprae. Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma lack peptidoglycan
cell walls and contain membrane sterols.
4. Discuss bacterial cell structures and their functions.
Answer: Cell envelope protects the bacterium. Cell wall provides structural protection. Plasma membrane controls
permeability, transport, and energy production. 70S ribosomes synthesize proteins. Inclusion bodies store
nutrients. Plasmids are extrachromosomal DNA that may carry resistance or virulence genes. Endospores are
resistant dormant survival structures. Glycocalyx aids protection/adherence; capsules resist phagocytosis and
desiccation; slime layers promote biofilms; flagella provide motility. Important inclusions: C. diphtheriae -
Babes-Ernst bodies; M. tuberculosis - Much granules; Y. pestis - bipolar bodies.
5. Discuss the Gram-staining procedure and function of each reagent.
Answer: 1) Crystal violet - primary stain. 2) Iodine - mordant. 3) Alcohol/acetone - decolorizer. 4) Safranin -
counterstain. Gram-positive organisms remain purple; Gram-negative organisms become pink/red.
6. Differentiate Ziehl-Neelsen from Kinyoun acid-fast staining.
Answer: Ziehl-Neelsen is the hot method: carbol fuchsin, heat, acid-alcohol, then methylene blue. Kinyoun is the
cold method: Kinyoun carbol fuchsin without heating, acid-alcohol, then counterstain. Acid-fast organisms are
red/pink. Mycobacterium is acid-fast; Nocardia is partially/weakly acid-fast.
7. Discuss physiologic requirements for bacterial growth.
Answer: Oxygen categories include aerobes, obligate anaerobes, facultative anaerobes, aerotolerant anaerobes,
and microaerophiles. Capnophiles require increased CO2. Nutritional classifications include autotroph/heterotroph,
phototroph/chemotroph, and lithotroph/organotroph. Temperature groups include psychrophiles, mesophiles,
thermophiles/hyperthermophiles, and extremophiles. pH groups include acidophiles, neutrophiles, and
alkalophiles. Moisture, salt, pressure, and growth factors also affect growth.
8. Explain the bacterial growth curve.
Answer: Lag phase: adjustment and biosynthesis without major division. Log/exponential phase: rapid division,
active metabolism, and greatest antimicrobial/radiation sensitivity. Stationary phase: division balances death as

9. Differentiate sterilization, disinfection, and decontamination.
Answer: Sterilization destroys all microbial life including spores. Disinfection destroys pathogenic microbes but not
necessarily all organisms/spores. Decontamination reduces/removes pathogens so items are safe to handle or
discard. Classic autoclaving: 121 C, 15 psi, 15-20 minutes. Other methods include boiling, pasteurization,
tyndallization, inspissation, dry heat, incineration, and chemical disinfectants.
10. Discuss Staphylococcus aureus.
Answer: Gram-positive cocci in clusters; catalase positive; coagulase positive; facultative anaerobe; salt tolerant;
often golden-yellow and beta-hemolytic. Diseases include food poisoning, TSS, SSSS, bacteremia/sepsis,
endocarditis, skin infections, osteomyelitis, and septic arthritis. Important factors include coagulase, hyaluronidase,
staphylokinase, lipase, DNase, beta-lactamase, enterotoxins, PVL, hemolysins, exfoliatins, TSST-1, and Protein A.
Tests include catalase, coagulase, mannitol fermentation, DNase, and tellurite glycine agar.
11. Differentiate S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and S. saprophyticus.
Answer: S. aureus is coagulase positive. Among coagulase-negative staphylococci, S. epidermidis is novobiocin
susceptible and associated with biofilms/devices; S. saprophyticus is novobiocin resistant and associated with
UTIs in young sexually active females.
12. Discuss MRSA and laboratory confirmation.
Answer: MRSA is methicillin-resistant S. aureus, resistant to methicillin, oxacillin, and nafcillin. Types include
HA-MRSA and CA-MRSA. Cefoxitin disk diffusion detects oxacillin resistance. PCR detection of the mecA gene is
the handout's gold-standard confirmation.
13. Discuss Streptococcus pyogenes.
Answer: Group A, Gram-positive, catalase-negative, beta-hemolytic streptococcus transmitted by droplets. Major
antiphagocytic factor: M protein. Products include Streptolysin O, Streptolysin S, DNases, streptokinase,
hyaluronidase, and pyrogenic toxins. Diseases include pharyngitis, scarlet fever, cellulitis, impetigo, rheumatic
fever, acute glomerulonephritis, and streptococcal TSS. Important tests: bacitracin susceptible, PYR positive, SXT
resistant.
14. Discuss Streptococcus agalactiae.
Answer: Group B Streptococcus. Habitat: female genital tract and lower GIT. Virulence: sialic-acid-containing
capsule. Causes neonatal meningitis, sepsis, pneumonia, UTI, and endocarditis. CAMP positive, hippurate
positive, bacitracin resistant, SXT resistant. Handout recommends screening pregnant women at 35-37 weeks.
15. Differentiate S. pneumoniae from viridans streptococci.
Answer: Both may be alpha-hemolytic. S. pneumoniae is lancet-shaped, encapsulated, optochin susceptible, bile
soluble, and Quellung positive. Viridans streptococci are optochin resistant, bile insoluble, do not grow in 6.5%
NaCl, are LAP positive and PYR negative, and are associated with subacute bacterial endocarditis.
16. Discuss Enterococci and their identification.
Answer: Former Group D streptococci and intestinal microbiota; important species include E. faecalis and E.
faecium. They can cause nosocomial UTI, endocarditis, bacteremia, and wound infection. They are Gram-positive
cocci in pairs/chains, PYR positive, bile-esculin positive, and grow in 6.5% NaCl and 40% bile.
17. Compare N. gonorrhoeae and N. meningitidis.
Answer: Both are Gram-negative diplococci and oxidase/catalase positive. N. gonorrhoeae is often intracellular in
PMNs, uses glucose only, and causes gonorrhea, urethritis, cervicitis, neonatal conjunctivitis, and purulent arthritis.
N. meningitidis uses glucose and maltose, colonizes the nasopharynx, has an important capsule, and causes
meningitis, meningococcemia, and Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome.

18. Discuss general characteristics of Enterobacteriaceae.
Answer: Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic, non-spore-forming bacilli that ferment glucose and reduce nitrate.
Generally catalase positive and oxidase negative. Most are motile by peritrichous flagella except the KSY group:
Klebsiella, Shigella, Yersinia. Most are intestinal microbiota, with Salmonella, Shigella, and Yersinia emphasized
as exceptions.
19. Discuss Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
Answer: Nonfermentative Gram-negative rod; obligate aerobe; non-spore-forming; motile by polar flagella;
catalase and oxidase positive; non-lactose fermenter. Colonies may be flat/spreading, beta-hemolytic, with
fruity/grape-like or corn-tortilla odor. Pigments: pyocyanin (blue-green), pyoverdin (yellow-green), pyorubin (red),
pyomelanin (brown/black). Associated with nosocomial respiratory infection, CF, UTI, wounds, swimmer's ear,
hot-tub syndrome, and ecthyma gangrenosum.
20. Compare Vibrio cholerae, Campylobacter jejuni, and Helicobacter pylori.
Answer: V. cholerae: curved/comma-shaped Gram-negative rod, oxidase positive, polar motility, alkaline
preference, cholera toxin, rice-water stool. C. jejuni: curved/seagull-wing Gram-negative rod, microaerophilic,
optimum about 42 C, associated with poultry, inflammatory/bloody diarrhea and Guillain-Barre syndrome. H. pylori:
spiral Gram-negative rod, microaerophilic, strongly urease positive, oxidase/catalase positive, associated with
Type B gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, and gastric carcinoma.
21. Compare tetanus and botulism.
Answer: Both involve Gram-positive anaerobic spore-forming Clostridium. C. tetani has a terminal spore and
produces tetanospasmin, blocking inhibitory neurotransmitters (GABA/glycine) and causing spastic paralysis,
trismus, and risus sardonicus. C. botulinum has subterminal spores and botulinum toxin, which blocks
acetylcholine release and causes diplopia, ptosis, dysphagia, and descending flaccid paralysis.
22. Discuss Mycobacterium tuberculosis and acid-fastness.
Answer: Mycobacteria have mycolic-acid-rich lipid cell walls, producing acid-fastness and poor Gram staining. M.
tuberculosis is slow-growing, acid-fast, may show cording, and causes pulmonary TB, Pott's disease, and
disseminated TB. MDR-TB is resistant to isoniazid and rifampin.
