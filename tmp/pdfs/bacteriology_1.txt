PART I: ESSAY / ORAL REVALIDA QUESTIONS
1. Explain the difference between safety, hazard, risk, and biosafety.
Answer: Safety refers to being protected from danger, risk, or injury. A hazard is something capable of causing
harm, while a risk refers to a situation in which a person may become exposed to that harm. A biological hazard
includes microorganisms or biological materials capable of causing disease, such as bacteria, viruses, fungi,
parasites, blood, body fluids, patient specimens, cultures, and contaminated laboratory materials. Biosafety
consists of practices, equipment, and procedures designed to protect laboratory personnel, the community, and the
environment from accidental exposure to infectious agents. Its major goals are to prevent infection and
contamination, ensure safe handling of microorganisms, and support accurate and reliable laboratory work.
Follow-up: Routes of transmission include ingestion, inoculation, tactile contamination, inhalation of infectious
material, and aerosol dispersion.
2. Explain the four Biosafety Levels.
Answer: BSL-1 handles low-risk organisms. BSL-2 handles organisms associated with human disease and requires
additional precautions. BSL-3 is used for higher-risk organisms, especially those presenting respiratory or inhalation
risk. BSL-4 provides the highest level of containment for extremely hazardous pathogens. As the biosafety level
increases, containment, PPE, facility design, and work practices become stricter.
3. Differentiate Class I, II, and III Biological Safety Cabinets.
Answer: Class I protects the user and environment but not the specimen. Class II protects the user, environment,
and product and is commonly used in hospital microbiology laboratories. Class III is completely enclosed, operates
under negative pressure, uses sealed gloves, and provides maximum containment. High-yield: Class I = personnel +
environment; Class II = personnel + product + environment; Class III = maximum containment.
4. What should you do following accidental laboratory exposure?
Answer: Stop work immediately, remove contaminated PPE, and wash the exposed area with soap and water. For
eye exposure, rinse with clean water for 15 minutes. Report the incident to the laboratory supervisor or biosafety
officer, complete an incident report, and follow the institution's post-exposure protocol.
5. Explain the principles of proper specimen collection.
Answer: Proper collection preserves organism viability while minimizing contamination. Collect during the acute
phase and, whenever possible, before antibiotics are administered. Select the correct anatomical site, use sterile
equipment and proper technique, collect an adequate quantity, use the appropriate container or transport medium,
label completely, and transport promptly. Delayed transport may kill the causative organism, allow contaminants to
overgrow, and lead to misdiagnosis.
6. Explain proper urine specimen collection.
Answer: For routine culture, collect a midstream clean-catch specimen in a sterile container. Clean the external
genitalia, begin urinating, discard the first portion, and collect the midstream portion without touching the inside of
the container or lid. Collection before antibiotics is preferred. First-morning urine may be ideal, and the specimen
should reach the laboratory promptly. Boric acid may preserve urine at room temperature for up to 24 hours.
7. Explain proper sputum collection and specimen acceptability.
Answer: An early-morning sputum specimen is preferred. The patient rinses the mouth with water and coughs
deeply to bring up lower respiratory material rather than saliva. Before collection, the patient should not eat, drink,
brush the teeth, smoke, or use mouthwash. About 1-2 teaspoons are collected in a sterile container. A poor-quality
sample with less than 25 WBCs and more than 10 epithelial cells per LPF suggests salivary contamination; a good
specimen should contain PMNs from the respiratory tract.
8. Why is CSF considered a critical specimen?

Answer: CSF may be collected for suspected meningitis or encephalitis and requires immediate processing. Blood
cultures should also be obtained. The reviewer lists approximately 1 mL for bacteria, 2 mL for fungi, and ideally 5-10
mL for AFB testing. CSF is treated as a STAT specimen, and refrigeration should be avoided when organisms such
as Neisseria meningitidis are suspected.
9. Give the major criteria for specimen rejection.
Answer: Mismatched patient information; unlabeled or mislabeled containers; QNS; unpreserved specimens over
two hours; leaking containers; specimens in inappropriate fixatives such as formalin; inappropriate anaerobic culture
requests; Foley catheter tips; dried specimens; poor-quality sputum; a single swab submitted for multiple tests; and
duplicate specimens within 24 hours.
10. Explain magnification, contrast, and resolution.
Answer: Magnification enlarges an image; total magnification equals ocular lens magnification multiplied by
objective lens magnification. Contrast makes the specimen distinguishable from the background. Resolution is the
ability to distinguish two closely positioned objects as separate.
11. Differentiate major microscopy methods.
Answer: Bright-field produces a dark specimen on a bright background and commonly requires staining. Dark-field
produces a bright specimen on a dark background. Phase-contrast permits examination of living, unstained
structures. Fluorescence microscopy uses UV light and fluorochromes. Electron microscopy uses electrons and
electromagnetic lenses, providing much higher resolution than light microscopy.
12. Differentiate sterilization from disinfection.
Answer: Sterilization destroys or removes all forms of microbial life, including bacterial spores. Disinfection
removes, inhibits, or kills microorganisms on inanimate objects but does not necessarily destroy all bacterial spores.
13. Explain the principle of autoclaving.
Answer: Autoclaving uses steam under pressure. A common condition in the reviewer is 121°C at 15 psi for 15-20
minutes for culture media, liquids, glass pipettes, and laboratory utensils. The biological indicator is Geobacillus
stearothermophilus.
14. Compare moist heat and dry heat sterilization.
Answer: Moist heat destroys microorganisms mainly through coagulation of enzymes and structural proteins and
degradation of nucleic acids. Examples include boiling, autoclaving, tyndallization, inspissation, and pasteurization.
Dry heat acts mainly through oxidative damage and protein denaturation and is useful for glassware, oils, petroleum
products, and powders. Bacillus atrophaeus is listed as the biological indicator for oven heating.
15. Explain tyndallization, inspissation, and pasteurization.
Answer: Tyndallization is intermittent sterilization using repeated heating over three successive days. Inspissation
uses heat to thicken and sterilize protein-rich media such as Lowenstein-Jensen medium. Pasteurization reduces
foodborne pathogens and spoilage organisms but does not reliably destroy endospores. LTH = 63°C for 30 minutes;
HTST = 72°C for 15 seconds; UHT = 140°C for 3 seconds.
16. Explain the Gram stain from principle to interpretation.
Answer: Remember V-I-A-S: Crystal Violet, Iodine, Alcohol/acetone-alcohol, Safranin. Crystal violet stains all cells
purple. Iodine acts as the mordant and forms the crystal violet-iodine complex. During decolorization, Gram-positive
bacteria retain the complex because of their thick peptidoglycan layer, while Gram-negative organisms lose it.
Safranin counterstains Gram-negative cells pink or red. Decolorization is the most critical step because
over-decolorization may cause false Gram-negative results and under-decolorization may cause false Gram-positive
results.
17. Compare Ziehl-Neelsen and Kinyoun staining.

Page 4
Answer: Both detect acid-fast organisms. Ziehl-Neelsen is the hot method because heat assists carbolfuchsin
penetration through the mycolic-acid-rich wall. Kinyoun is the cold method and requires no heating.
Auramine-Rhodamine fluorescence staining allows faster screening at lower magnification.
18. Explain capsule and endospore staining.
Answer: Capsule staining avoids heat fixation because heat can distort the capsule. India ink or nigrosin stains the
background, leaving the capsule as a clear halo. For endospores, the Schaeffer-Fulton method uses malachite
green as primary stain and safranin as counterstain; spores appear green and vegetative cells pink or red.
19. What is culture media and what are its applications?
Answer: Culture media are nutrient formulations designed to support microbial growth. They are used to isolate
pure cultures, identify causes of infection, estimate viable counts, test antimicrobial susceptibility, observe
biochemical reactions, transport specimens, produce antibiotics/vaccines/enzymes, and support research and
industrial applications.
20. Differentiate Blood Agar, Chocolate Agar, MacConkey, EMB, MSA, and TCBS.
Answer: Blood agar is enriched and differential and demonstrates hemolysis. Chocolate agar contains lysed RBCs
releasing X and V factors for fastidious organisms such as Neisseria and Haemophilus. MacConkey selects enteric
Gram-negative organisms and differentiates lactose fermentation. EMB differentiates enteric Gram-negative
bacteria; E. coli may show a metallic green sheen. MSA selects salt-tolerant staphylococci and differentiates
mannitol fermentation. TCBS is selective/differential for Vibrio; V. cholerae is yellow and V. parahaemolyticus green
or blue-green.
21. Explain the major mechanisms of antimicrobial action.
Answer: Major mechanisms include inhibition of cell-wall synthesis, disruption of cell membranes, inhibition of
protein synthesis, inhibition or damage of nucleic-acid synthesis, and inhibition of metabolic pathways. Examples
include beta-lactams and glycopeptides for cell walls; polymyxins and daptomycin for membranes; macrolides,
aminoglycosides, and tetracyclines for protein synthesis; fluoroquinolones and rifamycins for nucleic acids; and
sulfonamides/trimethoprim for folate metabolism.
22. Explain the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method.
Answer: Standardize the bacterial suspension to a 0.5 McFarland standard, inoculate Mueller-Hinton agar evenly,
apply antimicrobial disks, and incubate at approximately 35°C for 16-18 hours. Measure the diameter of each zone
of inhibition and interpret it using established breakpoints as susceptible, intermediate, resistant, or non-susceptible.
23. How would you identify an unknown Gram-positive coccus?
Answer: Start with Gram stain, then catalase. Catalase positive suggests Staphylococcus or Micrococcus; catalase
negative suggests Streptococcus or Enterococcus. For Staphylococcus, perform coagulase: positive suggests S.
aureus; negative suggests CoNS. For catalase-negative organisms, evaluate hemolysis and use tests such as
bacitracin, PYR, CAMP, optochin, bile solubility, bile esculin, and 6.5% NaCl growth.
24. Differentiate S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and S. saprophyticus.
Answer: All are catalase-positive staphylococci. S. aureus is coagulase positive, commonly beta-hemolytic, and
ferments mannitol. S. epidermidis and S. saprophyticus are coagulase negative. S. epidermidis is novobiocin
susceptible, while S. saprophyticus is novobiocin resistant.
25. Differentiate S. pyogenes and S. agalactiae.
Answer: Both are beta-hemolytic streptococci. S. pyogenes is Group A, bacitracin sensitive, and PYR positive. S.
agalactiae is Group B, bacitracin resistant, CAMP positive, and hippurate hydrolysis positive. CAMP positivity
produces arrowhead-shaped enhanced hemolysis near S. aureus.
26. Differentiate S. pneumoniae from viridans streptococci.

Page 5
Answer: Both can be alpha-hemolytic. S. pneumoniae is optochin susceptible, bile soluble, Quellung positive,
lancet-shaped, and encapsulated. Viridans streptococci are optochin resistant and bile insoluble.
27. Differentiate N. gonorrhoeae and N. meningitidis.
Answer: N. gonorrhoeae utilizes glucose only, while N. meningitidis utilizes glucose and maltose. CTA medium with
carbohydrate and phenol red can demonstrate utilization by a yellow reaction. N. gonorrhoeae may appear as
kidney- or coffee-bean-shaped diplococci within PMNs.
28. How would you identify Mycobacterium tuberculosis?
Answer: Use appropriate respiratory specimens such as sputum. Direct detection may use Ziehl-Neelsen, Kinyoun,
or Auramine-Rhodamine staining. Culture media include Lowenstein-Jensen and Middlebrook 7H10/7H11. M.
tuberculosis is a slow-growing nonchromogen, niacin positive, and nitrate positive. GeneXpert MTB/RIF detects M.
tuberculosis and rifampicin resistance.
29. Explain the X and V factor requirements of Haemophilus.
Answer: X factor is hemin/hematin and is heat stable. V factor is NAD/NADP and is heat labile. H. ducreyi requires
X only, H. parainfluenzae requires V only, and H. influenzae requires both X and V. Haemophilus may demonstrate
satellitism around S. aureus.
30. State the general characteristics of Enterobacteriaceae.
Answer: They are generally Gram-negative bacilli or coccobacilli, glucose fermenters, usually oxidase negative,
generally reduce nitrate to nitrite, and are facultatively anaerobic. Important exceptions noted in the reviewer include
Plesiomonas for oxidase and Klebsiella, Shigella, and Yersinia for motility patterns.
31. How do you differentiate fermenters from non-fermenters using O-F medium?
Answer: Fermenters acidify both open and closed tubes, turning both yellow. Oxidative non-fermenters acidify only
the open tube, so it becomes yellow while the closed tube stays green/blue. Asaccharolytic organisms do not make
either tube yellow.
32. Describe important laboratory characteristics of Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
Answer: P. aeruginosa is an oxidase-positive Gram-negative non-fermenter that uses glucose oxidatively. It
produces pyocyanin, may show green pigmentation from pyocyanin plus pyoverdin, grows on MacConkey and blood
agar, can form spreading beta-hemolytic colonies, has a characteristic odor, and may become mucoid in cystic
fibrosis patients.
33. Explain the principle of the catalase test.
Answer: Catalase breaks hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. The routine reagent is 3% hydrogen peroxide.
A positive reaction produces rapid continuous bubbling. Staphylococcus is catalase positive, while Streptococcus
and Enterococcus are catalase negative.
34. What does SIM medium test?
Answer: SIM stands for Sulfide, Indole, Motility. Blackening indicates H2S production. Indole detects tryptophan
breakdown, and motility is demonstrated by diffuse growth away from the stab line.

High-yield identification approach
For an unknown organism, follow a logical sequence: Gram stain → morphology → catalase/oxidase →
hemolysis or fermentation → selective/differential media → confirmatory biochemical test → final
identification. This demonstrates the reasoning behind bacterial identification rather than isolated memorization.
