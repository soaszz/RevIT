PART I - ESSAY / ORAL REVALIDA QUESTIONS
1. What is Quality Assessment in the hematology laboratory?
Answer: Quality Assessment is a system used to ensure reliable laboratory results. It includes quality assurance and
quality control. Quality control monitors accuracy and precision over time. The Total Testing Process has preanalytical,
analytical, and postanalytical phases, and errors can occur in any phase.
2. Explain the three phases of the Total Testing Process.
Answer: Preanalytical: patient identification, specimen collection, tube selection, order of draw, and processing.
Analytical: actual testing, instrument operation, assay performance, QC, and flags. Postanalytical: result reporting and
interpretation, critical-value reporting, and result transmission.
3. What should you do when an assay is out of control?
Answer: Reassay the control. If the problem persists, check the control material and reagents, then reassay. If unresolved,
recalibrate the instrument and determine whether repair or other corrective action is needed.
4. Explain proper patient identification before blood collection.
Answer: Use 3-way identification: obtain verbal identification including complete name and date of birth; check the
identification band; and compare the labeled specimen with the patient ID band before leaving the bedside.
5. Differentiate STAT, ASAP, timed, and routine specimens.
Answer: STAT means immediately and has highest priority. ASAP means as soon as possible. Timed specimens are
collected at a specified time. Routine specimens have no special urgency. Review-note priority: STAT, Timed, ASAP,
Routine.
6. Explain factors that can cause hemolysis.
Answer: A needle that is too small, pulling a syringe plunger too rapidly, forcefully expelling blood into a tube, vigorous
shaking or mixing, and collecting before alcohol dries can cause hemolysis.
7. What are important considerations in venipuncture?
Answer: Correct identification and positioning, vein selection, tourniquet use, cleansing and drying, needle insertion,
order of draw, tube mixing, bedside labeling, and prompt transport. In the H pattern, the median cubital vein is preferred,
followed by cephalic and basilic veins.
8. Why should blood not be collected above an IV site?
Answer: IV fluid can dilute or contaminate the specimen and produce erroneous results. Dextrose-containing fluid can
markedly increase measured glucose.
9. Explain capillary blood collection and when it is preferred.
Answer: Capillary blood comes from finger, heel, or earlobe puncture and contains arterial and venous blood with small
amounts of interstitial/intracellular fluid. It is useful in infants and young children, newborn screening, and some
point-of-care tests.
10. Why is the first drop of capillary blood usually discarded?
Answer: It may contain tissue fluid and residual alcohol that can alter the specimen. Follow manufacturer instructions
when a test specifically requires the first drop.
11. Explain the function of EDTA.
Answer: EDTA prevents coagulation by chelating calcium. It is preferred for CBC, morphology, and HbA1c. Too little may
allow clotting; excess EDTA can shrink RBCs and falsely decrease hematocrit and ESR.
12. Explain the importance of the correct blood-to-anticoagulant ratio in citrate tubes.

Answer: Sodium citrate preserves labile clotting factors for coagulation testing. Proper fill volume is essential; too little
blood can produce falsely prolonged clotting times.
13. Explain the major stages of hematopoiesis.
Answer: Yolk sac stage: early embryonic hematopoiesis. Hepatic stage: fetal liver is the major site, with spleen, thymus,
and lymph nodes contributing. Myeloid/bone marrow stage: marrow becomes the major site around the fifth to sixth
month of gestation and remains primary after birth.
14. What general morphologic changes occur as hematopoietic cells mature?
Answer: Cell size and nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio generally decrease. Chromatin condenses, nucleoli disappear, nuclear
shape becomes more characteristic, and cytoplasmic staining/granulation becomes more developed.
15. Explain erythropoiesis.
Answer: Erythropoiesis is RBC production and maturation. Tissue hypoxia increases erythropoietin, which stimulates
marrow. Erythroid precursors mature through normoblast stages to reticulocytes and then mature erythrocytes.
16. What is the primary stimulus for erythropoiesis?
Answer: Tissue hypoxia. It stimulates renal erythropoietin production, which increases bone marrow RBC production.
17. Explain hemoglobin structure.
Answer: Hemoglobin contains four globin polypeptide chains and four heme groups. Each heme contains iron that binds
oxygen. Globin-chain composition determines hemoglobin type.
18. Differentiate HbA1, HbA2, HbF, and embryonic hemoglobins.
Answer: HbA/HbA1 = alpha2 beta2; HbA2 = alpha2 delta2; HbF = alpha2 gamma2. Embryonic hemoglobins include
Gower 1 = zeta2 epsilon2, Gower 2 = alpha2 epsilon2, and Portland = zeta2 gamma2.
19. Explain the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve.
Answer: It shows oxygen pressure versus hemoglobin oxygen saturation and has a sigmoid shape due to cooperative
binding. A right shift means decreased oxygen affinity and easier tissue unloading; a left shift means increased affinity.
Temperature, pH, CO2, and 2,3-DPG affect the curve.
20. Differentiate methemoglobin, carboxyhemoglobin, and sulfhemoglobin.
Answer: Methemoglobin contains Fe3+ and cannot normally bind oxygen. Carboxyhemoglobin is hemoglobin bound to
carbon monoxide. Sulfhemoglobin is an irreversible sulfur-containing altered hemoglobin that may cause greenish
discoloration.
21. Explain hemoglobin electrophoresis.
Answer: It separates hemoglobins according to electrical charge. Alkaline electrophoresis is common; acid electrophoresis
helps distinguish variants that comigrate at alkaline pH. HPLC and capillary electrophoresis also identify variants.
22. Explain preparation and examination of a peripheral blood smear.
Answer: A good wedge smear is about two-thirds to three-fourths the slide length, smooth, finger-shaped, with a
feathered edge, visible lateral margins, and no holes or streaks. Examine at 10x for quality, 40x for differential
area/morphology, and 100x oil for detailed morphology and differential.
23. What is a Romanowsky stain?
Answer: A Romanowsky stain contains methylene blue and/or oxidation products plus a halogenated fluorescein dye
such as eosin. Examples include Wright, Giemsa, Wright-Giemsa, and Leishman stains.
24. Explain the major RBC morphological abnormalities.
Answer: Abnormalities involve size (anisocytosis, microcytosis, macrocytosis), shape (poikilocytes), hemoglobin content
(hypochromia/hyperchromia), inclusions, and distribution (rouleaux/agglutination).

25. Differentiate rouleaux from RBC agglutination.
Answer: Rouleaux is a stack-of-coins arrangement often linked to increased plasma proteins and can disperse with saline.
Agglutination is irregular antibody-mediated clumping, often IgM-related, and true agglutination remains after saline is
added.
26. Explain iron deficiency anemia.
Answer: Iron deficiency anemia results from inadequate iron for hemoglobin synthesis. Causes include inadequate intake,
increased demand, impaired absorption, and chronic blood loss. It is typically microcytic and hypochromic and may cause
fatigue, weakness, dyspnea, glossitis, angular cheilosis, koilonychia, and pica.
27. Differentiate iron deficiency anemia from anemia of chronic disease.
Answer: IDA: low serum iron, low ferritin, high TIBC, low transferrin saturation, decreased stores. Anemia of chronic
disease: low serum iron, increased ferritin, decreased TIBC, low-to-normal saturation, and iron stores that are present or
increased; hepcidin limits iron availability.
28. Explain megaloblastic anemia.
Answer: Defective DNA synthesis, commonly from vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, causes nuclear-cytoplasmic
asynchrony. Peripheral blood often shows macro-ovalocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils; pancytopenia can occur.
29. Differentiate vitamin B12 deficiency from folate deficiency.
Answer: Both can cause megaloblastic anemia, but neurologic manifestations strongly point to vitamin B12 deficiency.
B12 deficiency may take years to develop because stores are substantial; folate deficiency can develop more quickly.
30. Explain hemolytic anemia and its classification.
Answer: Hemolytic anemia occurs when RBC destruction exceeds marrow replacement. Classify it as hereditary/acquired,
intrinsic/extrinsic, acute/chronic, and intravascular/extravascular.
31. Differentiate intravascular and extravascular hemolysis.
Answer: Intravascular hemolysis occurs within circulation, often through fragmentation or complement-mediated
destruction. Extravascular hemolysis occurs mainly through macrophage removal, especially in the spleen.
32. Explain sickle cell disease.
Answer: Sickle cell disease results from valine replacing glutamic acid at beta-globin position 6. Deoxygenated HbS
polymerizes and deforms RBCs. HbSS is disease; HbAS is trait.
33. Explain thalassemia.
Answer: Thalassemia is an inherited quantitative globin-chain disorder with decreased production of one or more globin
chains. It causes reduced hemoglobin synthesis, microcytic hypochromic cells, target cells, and ineffective erythropoiesis.
34. Differentiate alpha and beta thalassemia.
Answer: Alpha thalassemia decreases alpha-chain production and can produce Hb Bart’s and HbH. Beta thalassemia
decreases beta-chain production; HbA2 and HbF measurements help distinguish it. Beta-thalassemia major causes severe
microcytic hypochromic anemia with markedly increased HbF.
35. Explain granulopoiesis.
Answer: Sequence: myeloblast, promyelocyte, myelocyte, metamyelocyte, band, mature granulocyte. The myelocyte is
the youngest stage identifiable by specific granules and the last stage capable of division.
36. Differentiate neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils.
Answer: Neutrophils usually have 2-5 lobes and pink-violet granules and are major phagocytes. Eosinophils are often
bilobed with orange-red granules and participate in allergy/parasitic responses. Basophils have dark purple-blue granules
containing mediators such as histamine and heparin.
37. Compare B cells, T cells, and NK cells.

Answer: B cells mature in bone marrow and mediate humoral immunity through antibody-producing plasma cells. T cells
mature in thymus and mediate cellular immunity. NK cells kill virus-infected or tumor cells without prior antigen-specific
sensitization.
38. Differentiate leukemoid reaction from CML.
Answer: Leukemoid reaction is reactive, usually with neutrophilia and left shift, and has increased LAP. CML is neoplastic,
has granulocytes at multiple maturation stages with eosinophilia/basophilia, decreased LAP, and is associated with the
Philadelphia chromosome.
39. Explain leukemia versus lymphoma.
Answer: Leukemia is a malignant hematopoietic proliferation that is initially systemic and involves marrow and often
blood. Lymphoma begins in lymphatic tissue and is initially localized; marrow/blood spread can create a leukemic phase.
40. Differentiate AML and ALL using cytochemistry.
Answer: AML generally shows MPO and Sudan Black B positivity. ALL is generally MPO/SBB negative and may express
TdT, reflecting immature lymphoid cells.

Memorize these first. They open the door to many common follow-up questions.
1. Explain hematopoiesis from embryo to adult.
2. Explain erythropoiesis and the role of EPO.
3. Differentiate HbA, HbA2, HbF, Gower 1, Gower 2, and Portland.
4. Explain the oxygen dissociation curve and right vs left shift.
5. Describe normal RBC morphology.
6. Enumerate and identify RBC poikilocytes.
7. Differentiate rouleaux and agglutination.
8. Explain iron deficiency anemia and its laboratory findings.
9. Differentiate IDA from anemia of chronic disease.
10. Explain megaloblastic anemia and differentiate B12 from folate deficiency.
11. Classify hemolytic anemia.
12. Differentiate intravascular and extravascular hemolysis.
13. Explain sickle cell disease.
14. Differentiate sickle cell disease and sickle cell trait.
15. Explain alpha and beta thalassemia.
16. Explain granulopoiesis from myeloblast to mature neutrophil.
17. Differentiate neutrophil, eosinophil, and basophil.
18. Differentiate B, T, and NK cells.
19. Differentiate leukemoid reaction and CML.
20. Explain leukemia vs lymphoma.
21. Differentiate AML and ALL using cytochemistry.
22. Explain CML and the Philadelphia chromosome.
23. Explain polycythemia vera.
24. Explain myelofibrosis and the significance of teardrop cells.
25. Explain CBC components and RBC indices.
26. Explain principles of automated hematology analyzers.
27. Explain specimen rejection and preanalytical errors.
28. Explain EDTA and consequences of incorrect blood-to-anticoagulant ratio.
29. Demonstrate proper venipuncture verbally from patient identification to specimen transport.
30. Explain peripheral blood smear preparation, staining, and examination.
